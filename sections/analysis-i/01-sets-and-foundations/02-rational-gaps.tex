\section{Rational gaps and irrational numbers}
\label{sec:analysis-i:rational-gaps}

\newthought{Counting is only one part of the story.} The rational numbers
are closed under the usual arithmetic operations, with division by zero
excluded. Yet even a simple geometric construction leads outside $\Q$.
The right triangle in \autoref{fig:unit-triangle} has legs of length one,
so its hypotenuse has square equal to two.
\begin{marginfigure}
  \centering
  \input{figures/foundations/unit-triangle}
  \caption[A unit right triangle.]{A unit right triangle produces the length $\sqrt{2}$.}
  \label{fig:unit-triangle}
\end{marginfigure}

\begin{proposition}
  The number $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Suppose $\sqrt{2}=p/q$, where $p,q\in\N$ have no common factor.
  Then $p^2=2q^2$, so $p^2$ is even. An odd integer has an odd square,
  hence $p=2r$ for some integer $r$. Substitution gives $q^2=2r^2$,
  so $q$ is even as well. This contradicts the choice of $p$ and $q$.
\end{proof}

The parity argument above is a special case of a general fact about
prime divisors. We write $d\mid m$ when $m=dk$ for some integer $k$.
A prime is an integer $p>1$ whose only positive divisors are $1$ and $p$.

\begin{lemma}[Euclid's lemma on prime divisors]
  \label{thm:analysis-i:euclid-prime-divisors}
  Let $p$ be prime and let $a,b\in\Z$. If $p$ divides a product $ab$,
  then it divides at least one factor.
  \[
    p\mid ab\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    p\mid a\quad\text{or}\quad p\mid b.
  \]
  In particular, $p\mid a^2$ implies $p\mid a$, since $a^2=a\cdot a$.
\end{lemma}
\marginnote{Euclid states this result in Book VII,
Proposition 30 of the \emph{Elements}~\cite{EuclidElementsVII30}.
His word ``measures'' means ``divides.'' His proof uses coprimality
and ratios of whole numbers, showing how ancient arithmetic already
isolated this decisive property of primes.}
\begin{proof}
  Suppose $p\nmid a$. By well-ordering, there is a least positive
  integer $d$ of the form $ua+vp$, with $u,v\in\Z$. Such positive
  integers exist because $p$ is one of them.

  Divide $a$ by $d$ with remainder, writing $a=qd+r$ with
  $0\le r<d$. The remainder $r=a-qd$ is another integer combination
  of $a$ and $p$. If $r>0$, it contradicts the minimality of $d$.
  Thus $r=0$ and $d\mid a$. The same argument applied to division
  of $p$ by $d$ gives $d\mid p$.

  Since $p$ is prime, $d$ is either $1$ or $p$. The second possibility
  would give $p\mid a$, contrary to our assumption. Hence $d=1$,
  so there are integers $u,v$ with $ua+vp=1$. Multiplying by $b$ gives
  \[
    b=uab+vpb.
  \]
  Both terms on the right are divisible by $p$, since $p\mid ab$.
  Therefore $p\mid b$, as required.
\end{proof}

Repeated application gives the consequence used in the root arguments,
\marginnote{The lemma is the key to uniqueness of
prime factorisation. A prime in one factorisation must divide a prime
in any other, so the two primes are equal. Cancel and repeat to match
all the factors. In \autoref{sec:analysis-i:roots-and-powers}, the same
principle detects prime divisors hidden inside powers, forcing the
common-factor contradiction in irrationality proofs.}
\[
  p\mid a^k\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p\mid a
  \qquad(k\in\N).
\]
Consequently, coprime integers have coprime positive powers. To see this,
every integer $d>1$ has a prime divisor, since its least divisor greater
than $1$ must be prime. Otherwise a proper factor would be a smaller
divisor greater than $1$. A common divisor $d>1$ of two powers would
therefore supply a prime dividing both bases, contradicting coprimality.

The prime hypothesis matters. For example, $6\mid2\cdot3$, although
$6\nmid2$ and $6\nmid3$.

The same divisibility argument extends to other primes and higher roots.
We take up that exercise in \autoref{sec:analysis-i:roots-and-powers},
after establishing existence of the roots themselves.

\subsection{A gap in the rational line}
We can describe the location of $\sqrt{2}$ without naming a rational number
at that location. Partition $\Q$ into
\begin{align*}
  L&=\{q\in\Q\mid q\leq0\text{ or }q^2<2\},\\
  U&=\{q\in\Q\mid q>0\text{ and }q^2>2\}.
\end{align*}
Both sets are nonempty, every rational belongs to one of them, and every
element of $L$ is less than every element of $U$. There is no rational
boundary between them. Indeed, if a rational boundary $c$ satisfied $c^2<2$,
a sufficiently small positive rational increment would leave its square
below two, contradicting its being a boundary. If $c^2>2$, a sufficiently
small rational decrement gives the opposite contradiction. The case $c^2=2$
has just been ruled out. Here a boundary must be positive, since $1\in L$.
The small increments can be checked directly from
$(c+h)^2=c^2+2ch+h^2$.

\historynote{Richard Dedekind traced his cuts to teaching calculus in
Z\"urich in 1858. He wanted an arithmetic account of continuity rather
than an appeal to a geometric line. His 1872 preface also acknowledges
related work by Heine and Cantor~\cite[Preface; \S\S~3--5]{Dedekind1872}.
This construction supplies the real-number foundation needed for limits. See \autoref{fig:portrait:dedekind}.}
\historyportrait{dedekind}{Richard Dedekind}{PortraitDedekind}

This is the idea behind a \emph{Dedekind cut}. Completing the rational line
means supplying its missing boundaries. An equivalent approach supplies
limits of rational Cauchy sequences. We will formulate both versions of
completeness in \autoref{sec:analysis-i:supremum}.
\marginnote{The condition $q\leq0$ in $L$ is necessary. The sets defined only
by $q^2<2$ and $q^2>2$ would put large negative rationals on the wrong side.}

\subsection{Limits can reveal further gaps}
The lecture also introduced $e$ as an irrational number obtained from
rational factorial partial sums. This is a different source of a gap from
the geometric example above. Its construction and irrationality proof
appear in \autoref{sec:analysis-i:denseness}, where monotone convergence
and geometric-tail estimates are available. For now it motivates the
question of which limits a number system contains.
