\section{Convergence and iteration}
\label{sec:analysis-i:convergence-preview}

\newthought{A sequence may approach a number that its terms never reach.}
This is why a field containing all the rational numbers can still be
missing limits. We now make the meaning of ``approach'' precise.

\subsection{Distance and the triangle inequality}
For a real number $x$, define
\begin{marginfigure}
  \centering
  \input{figures/foundations/absolute-value}
  \caption[The absolute value function.]{The graph of $y=|x|$ consists
    of the ray $y=-x$ for $x\leq0$ and the ray $y=x$ for $x\geq0$.
    Its vertex is the origin.}
  \label{fig:absolute-value}
\end{marginfigure}
\[
  |x|=\begin{cases}
    x,&x\geq0,\\
    -x,&x<0.
  \end{cases}
\]
\autoref{fig:absolute-value} shows the graph of $y=|x|$.
It measures distance from zero, so $|x-y|$ measures distance between
$x$ and $y$. We have $|x|\geq0$, with equality exactly when $x=0$,
and $-|x|\leq x\leq|x|$.

\begin{proposition}
  \label{thm:analysis-i:absolute-value-inequalities}
  For real $x,y$,
  \[
    |xy|=|x|\,|y|,\qquad |x+y|\leq|x|+|y|.
  \]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  If either factor is zero, the product identity is immediate.
  Otherwise, writing each factor as its sign times its positive absolute
  value shows that the absolute value of the product is $|x|\,|y|$.
  For the second assertion, add the inequalities
  $-|x|\leq x\leq|x|$ and $-|y|\leq y\leq|y|$ to obtain
  \[
    -(|x|+|y|)\leq x+y\leq |x|+|y|.
  \]
  The definition of absolute value now gives the triangle inequality.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Convergence and its elementary rules}
\begin{definition}
  A sequence $(x_n)$ of real numbers \emph{converges} to $L\in\R$ if,
  for every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $N\in\N$ such that
  \[
    n\geq N\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |x_n-L|<\varepsilon.
  \]
  We write $x_n\to L$ or $\lim_{n\to\infty}x_n=L$.
\end{definition}
The index $N$ may depend on $\varepsilon$. After that index, every term
lies in the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood $(L-\varepsilon,L+\varepsilon)$.
For convergence \emph{in $\Q$}, the limit must belong to $\Q$ as well.
We will later construct rational sequences with irrational real limits.

We will use two elementary consequences of this definition. Limits are
unique, since if $L\ne M$, the disjoint neighborhoods of radius
$|L-M|/3$ cannot both contain all sufficiently late terms. Also, if
$x_n\leq y_n$, $x_n\to x$, and $y_n\to y$, then $x\leq y$.
Otherwise neighborhoods of radius $(x-y)/3$ contradict $x_n\leq y_n$.

We also need the basic rules for calculating a limit.
\begin{proposition}
  \label{thm:analysis-i:limit-laws}
  If $x_n\to L$ and $y_n\to M$, then
  \[
    x_n+y_n\to L+M,\qquad x_ny_n\to LM.
  \]
  If $M\ne0$, then $y_n\ne0$ for all sufficiently large $n$, and
  $x_n/y_n\to L/M$ on that tail.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  For the sum, the triangle inequality bounds the error by
  $|x_n-L|+|y_n-M|$. Make each term smaller than $\varepsilon/2$.
  For the product, convergence gives $|x_n|\leq|L|+1$ on a tail, and
  \[
    |x_ny_n-LM|
      \leq (|L|+1)|y_n-M|+|M|\,|x_n-L|.
  \]
  On a sufficiently late tail, the two errors are smaller than
  $\varepsilon/(2(|L|+1))$ and $\varepsilon/(2(|M|+1))$, respectively.
  The displayed bound is then smaller than $\varepsilon$.

  If $M\ne0$, choose a tail with $|y_n-M|<|M|/2$.
  The triangle inequality gives $|y_n|>|M|/2>0$ there. Hence
  \[
    \left|\frac1{y_n}-\frac1M\right|
      =\frac{|y_n-M|}{|y_n|\,|M|}
      \leq\frac{2|y_n-M|}{|M|^2}\longrightarrow0.
  \]
  Apply the product rule to $x_n$ and $1/y_n$ to obtain the quotient rule.
\end{proof}
Changing or discarding finitely many terms does not affect a limit.
In particular, $x_{n+1}\to L$ whenever $x_n\to L$.
We will also use the \emph{squeeze principle}. If
$u_n\leq v_n\leq w_n$ and $u_n,w_n\to L$, then $v_n\to L$,
because sufficiently late $u_n$ and $w_n$ both lie between
$L-\varepsilon$ and $L+\varepsilon$.

\subsection{Newton's idea}
For the calculus motivation here, $f'(x)$ denotes the limiting slope
$\lim_{h\to0}(f(x+h)-f(x))/h$. The two slopes needed in our examples
follow from the identities
\[
  \frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}=2x+h,\qquad
  \frac{1/(x+h)-1/x}{h}=-\frac1{x(x+h)}.
\]
Thus $(x^2)'=2x$ and $(1/x)'=-1/x^2$ for $x\ne0$.
These identities supply the tangent lines we use without requiring a
general differentiation theory.

Suppose we want to solve $f(x)=0$. At a current approximation $x_n$ with
$f'(x_n)\ne0$, replace the graph by its tangent line
\[
  y=f(x_n)+f'(x_n)(x-x_n).
\]
Its horizontal intercept supplies the next approximation,
\begin{equation}
  x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}.
  \label{eq:analysis-i:newton}
\end{equation}
\autoref{fig:newton-square-root} illustrates one step. This is a
motivation from calculus; a general convergence theorem for Newton's
method is not being assumed.
\begin{marginfigure}
  \centering
  \input{figures/foundations/newton-square-root}
  \caption[One Newton step for a square root.]{For $f(x)=x^2-2$, the tangent at $x_0=2$ meets the axis at
    $x_1=3/2$. The parabola is shown for $x\geq0$.}
  \label{fig:newton-square-root}
\end{marginfigure}

For $f(x)=x^2-a$, where $a>0$, \autoref{eq:analysis-i:newton} becomes
\[
  x_{n+1}=\frac12\left(x_n+\frac{a}{x_n}\right).
\]
A positive limit $L$, if it exists, must satisfy $L^2=a$ by the limit
laws. A fixed-point calculation alone does not establish that such a
limit exists. We return to its convergence proof and the iteration
exercises in \autoref{sec:analysis-i:roots-and-powers}, after proving
monotone convergence and constructing the required roots.

\subsection{What kind of limit can a recurrence produce?}
The lecture also considered
\[
  x_{n+1}=a+\frac{1}{x_n},\qquad a\in\N,\quad x_0>0.
\]
If this sequence converges, its limit is positive because $x_n>a$ for
$n\geq1$. The limit therefore satisfies
\[
  L^2-aL-1=0,\qquad L=\frac{a+\sqrt{a^2+4}}{2}.
\]
It is algebraic and irrational. Indeed, the rational root theorem would
force a rational root of this monic polynomial to be an integer dividing
$1$, and neither $1$ nor $-1$ is a root for $a\geq1$.
Repeated substitution also suggests a continued fraction. This example
identifies a possible limit; it does not by itself prove convergence.

Limits of rational sequences need not be algebraic. The factorial partial
sums will give $e$ in \autoref{sec:analysis-i:denseness}.
Another lecture example is Euler's identity
\[
  \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6},
\]
stated here without proof~\cite{Waldschmidt2017}.
The number $\pi^2/6$ is transcendental, since a nonzero rational polynomial
vanishing there would, after substituting $X^2/6$, give a nonzero rational
polynomial vanishing at $\pi$.
