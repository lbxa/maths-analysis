\section{Power series}
\label{sec:analysis-i:power-series}

A power series uses a variable as the base of each term. For each fixed
value of that variable, it is an ordinary number series, so the tests
from \autoref{sec:analysis-i:comparison-tests} apply.

\begin{definition}
  A \emph{power series centred at zero} has the form
  \[
    \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_nx^n,
  \]
  where the coefficients $c_n$ are fixed real numbers.
  Its \emph{radius of convergence} is a number $R\in[0,+\infty]$
  such that the series converges absolutely for $|x|<R$ and diverges
  for $|x|>R$. The points $x=\pm R$, when $0<R<\infty$, must be
  examined separately.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
  \label{thm:analysis-i:power-series-radius}
  Every real power series centred at zero has a radius of convergence
  $R\in[0,+\infty]$ with the properties in the preceding definition.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  At $x=0$ the constant term is $c_0$ and every other term vanishes,
  so the series converges there. Suppose it converges at some
  $x_0\ne0$. Its terms tend to zero by
  \autoref{thm:analysis-i:series-term-test}, so there is a number $B>0$
  such that $|c_nx_0^n|\le B$ for every $n\ge0$. If $|x|<|x_0|$, then
  \[
    |c_nx^n|\le B\left(\frac{|x|}{|x_0|}\right)^n.
  \]
  The geometric majorant converges. Thus convergence at any nonzero
  point implies absolute convergence at every point closer to zero.

  Let $S$ consist of the nonnegative values $r$ where the power series
  converges absolutely. This set contains $0$.
  If $S$ is bounded above, let $R=\sup S$. Otherwise let $R=+\infty$.
  If $|x|<R$, there is an $r\in S$ with $|x|<r$. The preceding
  comparison then proves absolute convergence at $x$. For $R=0$
  this interior condition is empty, while convergence at zero remains.

  If $R<\infty$ and the series converged at a point with $|x|>R$,
  choose $t$ with $R<t<|x|$. The same comparison would give absolute
  convergence at $t$, so $t\in S$, contradicting that $R$ is an upper
  bound. The series therefore diverges for $|x|>R$. This proves all
  the required properties. Boundary behaviour when $0<R<\infty$
  still requires separate tests.
\end{proof}


An infinite radius means absolute convergence for every real $x$.


\subsection{Computing the radius and controlling interior intervals}
For the series $\sum c_nx^n$, set
\[
  L=\limsup_{n\to\infty}|c_n|^{1/n}.
\]
Then
\[
  R=\frac1L,\qquad \frac10=+\infty,\qquad \frac1{+\infty}=0.
\]
Indeed, for $x\ne0$ the upper limit of the $n$th roots of the terms
is $|x|L$. A value below $1$ gives absolute convergence by
\autoref{thm:analysis-i:root-test}. A value above $1$ gives infinitely
many terms whose absolute values exceed a fixed geometric sequence
with ratio greater than $1$, so the terms fail to tend to zero.
These arguments also cover $L=0$ and $L=+\infty$.
If the coefficients are eventually nonzero and
$|c_{n+1}/c_n|\to\ell$, the ratio test gives the alternative formula
$R=1/\ell$, with the same conventions. Neither formula decides the
finite endpoints.

\begin{proposition}[Uniform convergence inside the radius]
  \label{thm:analysis-i:power-series-local-uniform}
  If a power series has radius $R>0$, it converges absolutely and
  uniformly on every $[-r,r]$ with $0\le r<R$. Its sum is continuous
  on $(-R,R)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  For $0<r<R$, set $M_n=|c_n|r^n$. The numerical series $\sum M_n$
  converges by the radius property, and $|c_nx^n|\le M_n$ for every
  $x\in[-r,r]$. Apply \autoref{thm:analysis-i:weierstrass-m-test}.
  At $r=0$ only the constant term remains. Each partial sum is a
  polynomial, hence continuous, so
  \autoref{thm:analysis-i:uniform-limit-continuity} makes the sum
  continuous on every such interval. Every point of $(-R,R)$ lies
  in the interior of one of them.
\end{proof}
Uniform convergence on each smaller closed interval need not mean
uniform convergence on the whole open interval. The geometric-series
example following \autoref{thm:analysis-i:weierstrass-m-test} shows this.


\subsection{The geometric power series}

The series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n$ has radius $R=1$, since
\autoref{sec:analysis-i:number-series} proves that it converges exactly
for $|x|<1$. Neither endpoint is included. At $x=1$ every term is $1$.
At $x=-1$ the terms alternate between $1$ and $-1$. In both cases the
terms fail to tend to zero.

\subsection{Algebraic and transcendental functions}
\begin{definition}[Algebraic and transcendental functions]
  \label{thm:analysis-i:algebraic-function}
  A real-valued function $f$ on a nonempty open interval $I$ is
  \emph{algebraic} if there is a nonzero polynomial $P(X,Y)$ with
  real coefficients such that
  \[
    \forall x\in I\quad P(x,f(x))=0.
  \]
  A function for which no such polynomial exists is
  \emph{transcendental}.
\end{definition}
For a polynomial function $f(x)=p(x)$, take $P(X,Y)=Y-p(X)$.
For $f(x)=p(x)/q(x)$ on an interval where $q$ never vanishes, take
$P(X,Y)=q(X)Y-p(X)$. The root function $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ on $(0,\infty)$
is algebraic because $f(x)^2-x=0$.

The classification concerns a polynomial relation between input and
output. Here the coefficients may be arbitrary real numbers. Thus even
the constant function $f(x)=\pi$ is algebraic, using $Y-\pi=0$,
although $\pi$ is a transcendental number over $\Q$.
The exponential and logarithm below are transcendental functions on
their full real domains, as we will prove after constructing them.


\subsection{The exponential series}

Consider the polynomials and their limiting series
\[
  E_N(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{x^k}{k!},\qquad
  E(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^k}{k!}.
\]
For fixed $x\ne0$, the absolute successive-term ratio is
\[
  \frac{|x|^{k+1}/(k+1)!}{|x|^k/k!}
  =\frac{|x|}{k+1}\longrightarrow0.
\]
The ratio test proves absolute convergence for every such $x$.
At $x=0$, only the constant term remains, so $E(0)=1$.
Thus this series has infinite radius of convergence and $E_N(x)\to E(x)$
for every fixed real $x$. We write $E(x)=e^x$.
The remaining properties follow from uniform convergence and
series multiplication, as shown below. At $x=1$ the sum is the earlier
factorial-series definition of $e$.

\subsection{Exponential and logarithmic properties}

\begin{theorem}[The exponential function]
  \label{thm:analysis-i:exponential-properties}
  The series $E(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n/n!$ defines a continuous,
  strictly increasing bijection from $\R$ onto $(0,\infty)$. It satisfies
  \[
    E(0)=1,\qquad E(1)=e,\qquad E(x+y)=E(x)E(y),
  \]
  and
  \[
    E(-x)=\frac1{E(x)},\qquad
    \lim_{x\to\infty}E(x)=+\infty,\qquad
    \lim_{x\to-\infty}E(x)=0.
  \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  On every $[-r,r]$, the bounds $|x^n/n!|\le r^n/n!$ have a
  convergent numerical sum by the ratio test. By
  \autoref{thm:analysis-i:weierstrass-m-test} and
  \autoref{thm:analysis-i:uniform-limit-continuity}, $E$ is
  continuous there, hence on $\R$. Its values at zero and one follow
  directly from the series and the earlier definition of $e$.

  Both exponential series converge absolutely. By
  \autoref{thm:analysis-i:absolute-cauchy-product}, their product is
  the series whose $n$th coefficient is
  \[
    \sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{x^ky^{n-k}}{k!(n-k)!}
      =\frac1{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk x^ky^{n-k}
      =\frac{(x+y)^n}{n!}.
  \]
  The finite binomial theorem therefore gives $E(x)E(y)=E(x+y)$.
  Taking $y=-x$ gives $E(x)E(-x)=1$, so neither factor is zero.
  Also $E(x)=E(x/2)^2>0$.

  If $h>0$, the series gives $E(h)\ge1+h>1$. Hence
  $E(x+h)=E(x)E(h)>E(x)$, proving strict increase.
  For $x\ge0$, the same bound $E(x)\ge1+x$ gives divergence to
  $+\infty$. The reciprocal identity gives the limit zero at
  $-\infty$. Continuity and \autoref{thm:analysis-i:intermediate-value}
  now show that every positive value is attained, and strict increase
  gives uniqueness.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Natural logarithm and positive-base powers]
  \label{thm:analysis-i:logarithm-real-powers}
  The \emph{natural logarithm} is the inverse function
  $\ln:(0,\infty)\to\R$ of $E$. We write $e^x=E(x)$, and for a
  positive base $a$ and real exponent $r$ define
  \[
    a^r=E(r\ln a).
  \]
\end{definition}
The inverse is continuous by
\autoref{thm:analysis-i:continuous-inverse}, applied on closed
subintervals of $\R$, and is strictly increasing. Its defining identities
and endpoint limits are
\[
  \begin{gathered}
    \ln(e^x)=x,\qquad e^{\ln t}=t\quad(t>0),\\
    \ln1=0,\qquad \ln e=1,\\
    \lim_{t\to0^+}\ln t=-\infty,\qquad
    \lim_{t\to\infty}\ln t=+\infty.
  \end{gathered}
\]
For the endpoint limits, compare $t$ with $E(A)$ for any fixed real
$A$ and use the strict increase of the inverse.

For $u,v>0$, the addition law gives
$E(\ln u+\ln v)=uv$. Applying the inverse, and then using the
reciprocal identity, proves
\[
  \ln(uv)=\ln u+\ln v,\qquad
  \ln(u/v)=\ln u-\ln v.
\]
The definition of powers consequently gives, for $a,b>0$ and
$r,s\in\R$,
\[
  \begin{gathered}
    a^0=1,\qquad a^{r+s}=a^ra^s,\qquad
    \ln(a^r)=r\ln a,\\
    (a^r)^s=a^{rs},\qquad (ab)^r=a^rb^r.
  \end{gathered}
\]
For rational $r=m/n$, the positive number $E(r\ln a)$ has $n$th
power $a^m$, so uniqueness of positive roots identifies it with the
earlier rational power. The real-exponent definition therefore extends
that construction. Continuity of $E$ and $\ln$ makes $a^r$ continuous
in each variable on its domain. As a function of $r$ it is strictly
increasing for $a>1$, constant for $a=1$, and strictly decreasing for
$0<a<1$, according to the sign of $\ln a$.

\begin{example}[Why the exponential and logarithm are transcendental]
  Suppose a nonzero polynomial relation held for $E$ on $\R$. Write it
  as $\sum_{k=0}^{m}p_k(x)E(x)^k=0$, with $p_m$ a nonzero polynomial.
  A relation with $m=0$ would already make a nonzero polynomial vanish
  everywhere, which is impossible. For $m\ge1$, division by $E(x)^m$
  gives
  \[
    p_m(x)=-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}p_k(x)E(-(m-k)x).
  \]
  Each term on the right tends to zero as $x\to\infty$. Indeed, if
  $d$ is the degree of a nonzero $p_k$, choose an integer $N>d$.
  For $x\ge1$, there is a constant $C$ with $|p_k(x)|\le Cx^d$,
  while the positive-term series gives, for $c=m-k>0$,
  \[
    E(cx)\ge\frac{(cx)^N}{N!},\qquad
    |p_k(x)|E(-cx)\le\frac{CN!}{c^N}x^{d-N}\longrightarrow0.
  \]
  This would make $p_m(x)\to0$, which no nonzero polynomial can do
  as $x\to\infty$. Thus $E$ is transcendental on $\R$.

  If a nonzero polynomial $P$ satisfied $P(t,\ln t)=0$ for all $t>0$,
  substituting $t=E(x)$ would give $P(E(x),x)=0$ for every real $x$.
  Interchanging the two polynomial variables produces a forbidden
  polynomial relation for $E$. Hence $\ln$ is transcendental on
  $(0,\infty)$ as well. This concerns the whole function. For example,
  its individual value $E(0)=1$ is rational.
\end{example}

\subsection{A family with different endpoints}

Let $s\ge0$ be a real number and consider
\[
  F_s(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n^s}.
\]
As in the $p$-series example, real powers use their usual exponent and
order laws. For $x\ne0$, the $n$th root of the absolute value of the
$n$th term is
\[
  \left|\frac{x^n}{n^s}\right|^{1/n}
  =\frac{|x|}{(n^{1/n})^s}\longrightarrow |x|.
\]
To justify the denominator's limit using the sequence results already
available, choose an integer $m\ge s$. Since $n^{1/n}\ge1$,
\[
  1\le(n^{1/n})^s\le(n^{1/n})^m\longrightarrow1.
\]
The last limit follows from \autoref{thm:analysis-i:root-sequence-limits}
and the product rule, and the squeeze principle gives the desired limit.

For $|x|<1$, the root test proves absolute convergence, with $x=0$
immediate. For $|x|>1$, choose $1<r<|x|$. The root values eventually
exceed $r$, so $|x^n/n^s|>r^n$ for every sufficiently large $n$.
The terms do not tend to zero and the series diverges. Therefore $R=1$
for every $s\ge0$.

\begin{marginfigure}
  \centering
  \input{figures/series/power-series-endpoints}
  \caption[Three endpoint patterns for radius one.]{All three cases have radius $1$. Open or filled endpoint
    markers record the separate tests at $-1$ and $1$. The radius
    alone does not settle them.}
  \label{fig:power-series-endpoints}
\end{marginfigure}

At $x=1$, this is the $p$-series, which converges exactly when $s>1$.
At $x=-1$, it is $\sum(-1)^n/n^s$, which converges for $s>0$ by
\autoref{ex:analysis-i:alternating-power-series}. For $s=0$, the terms
do not tend to zero. Accordingly the intervals of convergence are
\[
  \begin{array}{c|c}
    s=0 & (-1,1)\\[3pt]
    0<s\le1 & [-1,1)\\[3pt]
    s>1 & [-1,1].
  \end{array}
\]
\autoref{fig:power-series-endpoints} keeps the three cases distinct.
