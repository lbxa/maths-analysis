\section{Series of functions}
\label{sec:analysis-i:series-of-functions}

% Editorial completion authorised by the HW06 topic-coverage request.
% This material is not a transcription of an additional lecture source.

A series of functions is studied through its partial sums, just as a
series of numbers is. The input remains a variable throughout the
argument.

\begin{definition}[Series of functions]
  \label{thm:analysis-i:function-series}
  Let $f_n:D\to\R$ for $n=0,1,2,\ldots$, and put
  \[
    S_N(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}f_n(x).
  \]
  The series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_n(x)$ converges \emph{pointwise} on
  $D$ if $S_N(x)$ has a finite limit $S(x)$ for every $x\in D$.
  It converges \emph{absolutely at $x$} if
  $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|f_n(x)|<\infty$.
\end{definition}

Uniform convergence of the series means uniform convergence of the
sequence $(S_N)$, using the definition in
\autoref{sec:analysis-i:uniform-convergence}. Its error is the entire
remaining tail,
\[
  S(x)-S_N(x)=\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}f_n(x).
\]
Uniform convergence of the individual terms $f_n$ to zero is necessary
for uniform convergence of the series, but is insufficient by itself.
Indeed, if $S_N\to S$ uniformly, then for $N\ge1$,
\[
  |f_N(x)|\le |S_N(x)-S(x)|+|S_{N-1}(x)-S(x)|,
\]
and both errors become uniformly small.
For example, the constant functions $f_n(x)=1/(n+1)$ tend uniformly to
zero on every nonempty domain, whereas their series is harmonic and
diverges at every input.

The uniform Cauchy criterion and the majorant test in
\autoref{sec:analysis-i:cauchy-criterion-for-uniform-convergence} will
control these tails without requiring an explicit formula for $S$.
