\section{Taylor's theorem}
\label{sec:analysis-i:taylor-theorem}

The following statement records the tool needed for the exponential
interpretation of the factorial series. Its proof will be developed with
the differentiation lectures.

\begin{theorem}[Taylor's theorem with Lagrange remainder]
  Suppose $f$ has $n+1$ continuous derivatives on an interval containing
  the segment between $a$ and $x$, where $n\geq0$ and $x\ne a$.
  There is a point $\xi$ strictly between $a$ and $x$ such that
  \[
    f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k
      +\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}.
  \]
\end{theorem}

\subsection{The factorial series and the exponential function}
\autoref{ex:hw02:factorial-sequence} identified the supremum of the
factorial partial sums using their series definition of $e$.
Once the exponential function is available, Taylor's theorem gives
another identification of the same limit.

The exponential function has value $1$ at zero, and each derivative
equals the same continuous function. Apply Taylor's theorem about $0$
and evaluate at $1$ to obtain
\[
  \exp(1)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac1{k!}
    +\frac{\exp(\xi_n)}{(n+1)!},\qquad 0<\xi_n<1.
\]
Since $\exp$ is increasing, the remainder satisfies
\[
  0<\exp(1)-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac1{k!}
    \leq\frac{\exp(1)}{(n+1)!}\longrightarrow0.
\]
The factorials tend to infinity, for example because $(n+1)!\geq2^n$.
Thus $\exp(1)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}1/k!=e$. This later calculus argument
is independent of the elementary construction of the series limit in
\autoref{sec:analysis-i:denseness}.
