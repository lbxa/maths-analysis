\section{Cauchy sequences}
\label{sec:analysis-i:cauchy-sequences}

\begin{definition}
  A real sequence $(a_n)$ is \emph{Cauchy} if
  \[
    \forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\in\N\quad
    \forall m,n\ge N\quad |a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon.
  \]
\end{definition}

Here $m,n\ge N$ means $m\ge N$ and $n\ge N$, with both indices
chosen independently from the same tail. We can equivalently require
the inequality only for $m>n\ge N$. Indeed, exchanging the indices
does not change the distance, since $|a_m-a_n|=|a_n-a_m|$, and
the case $m=n$ gives $0<\varepsilon$. In either form, every pair of
distinct tail terms must be close, however far apart their indices are.

Convergence compares late terms with a proposed limit. The Cauchy
condition compares late terms with one another, so it can be checked
without knowing the limit in advance.
\autoref{fig:cauchy-tail-diameter} shows the whole tail confined to a
small interval. A smaller tolerance may require a larger starting index;
the condition controls every pair of terms after that index.

\begin{theorem}[Cauchy completeness of the real numbers]
  \label{thm:analysis-i:cauchy-completeness}
  A real sequence converges if and only if it is Cauchy.
\end{theorem}

If $a_n\to L$, choose $N$ so that $|a_n-L|<\varepsilon/2$ for
$n\ge N$. The triangle inequality then gives
$|a_m-a_n|\le|a_m-L|+|a_n-L|<\varepsilon$.
The converse uses completeness, as proved in
\autoref{sec:analysis-i:completeness-equivalences}. A Cauchy sequence
is bounded, hence has a convergent subsequence; its Cauchy property
then forces every sufficiently late term towards the same limit.
We will apply this theorem to partial sums of series.
