% Homework 03, question 5.
\begin{proof}[Solution]
  For part (a), the length of each interval is the difference between
  its endpoints, so
  \[
    |I_n(\varepsilon)|
      =(q_n+\varepsilon2^{-n})-(q_n-\varepsilon2^{-n})
      =2\varepsilon2^{-n}.
  \]
  The finite geometric sum gives
  \[
    \sum_{n=0}^{N}|I_n(\varepsilon)|
      =2\varepsilon\sum_{n=0}^{N}2^{-n}
      =4\varepsilon\bigl(1-2^{-(N+1)}\bigr).
  \]
  Letting $N\to\infty$, we obtain
  \[
    \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|I_n(\varepsilon)|=4\varepsilon.
  \]
  In particular, the requested upper bound holds. Given any $\delta>0$,
  choosing $0<\varepsilon<\delta/4$ covers every point of $Q$ with
  intervals whose total length is less than $\delta$.

  For part (b), this explains the word ``sparse'' in terms of covering
  length. It does not mean that the rationals are separated by gaps.
  Rational density gives a point of $Q$ in every open subinterval of
  $[0,1]$, so the closure of $Q$ is the whole interval. Nor does the
  covering estimate alone prove that $Q$ is incomplete. A singleton
  is complete and also admits covers of arbitrarily small length.

  To prove incompleteness, we must instead exhibit a Cauchy sequence
  in $Q$ with no limit in $Q$. Let $\alpha=\sqrt{2}/2$. Then
  $0<\alpha<1$, and $\alpha$ is irrational, since otherwise
  $\sqrt{2}=2\alpha$ would be rational. Take the decimal approximations
  \[
    x_n=\frac{\lfloor10^n\alpha\rfloor}{10^n},\qquad n\geq1.
  \]
  Each $x_n$ belongs to $Q$, and the defining inequalities for the
  floor function give
  \[
    0\leq\alpha-x_n<10^{-n}.
  \]
  Given $\eta>0$, choose $N$ so that $2\cdot10^{-N}<\eta$. For
  $m,n\geq N$, the triangle inequality then gives
  \[
    |x_m-x_n|
      \leq|x_m-\alpha|+|x_n-\alpha|
      <2\cdot10^{-N}<\eta.
  \]
  Thus $(x_n)$ is Cauchy. The same approximation bound shows that
  $x_n\to\alpha$ in $\R$. By uniqueness of limits, it cannot converge
  to a point of $Q$. This missing limit proves that $Q$ is incomplete.
  Since $Q$ is dense in $[0,1]$ and this closed interval is complete,
  $[0,1]$ is its completion under the usual distance.
\end{proof}
